Hanwha's Philly Shipyard lands its first US defense job: two 'Golden Dome' missile-tracking ships

Open in the Stacks app → Read the original ↗

Meru walks through why a shipyard Hanwha bought for about $100M just got interesting. On July 17, TOTE Services and Hanwha's Philly Shipyard were picked to build the US Missile Defense Agency's next Missile Range Instrumentation Vessels (MRIVs).

What do these ships do?

MRIVs track a missile's speed, altitude and trajectory and feed the data back. Today's fleet is two ancient boats (built 1965 and 1970); the two replacements are reportedly about $2B for the pair. They will support Trump's 'Golden Dome' homeland missile shield, with first delivery targeted for 2030. Naval News reported that the first ship is named Golden Defender and that the two retiring boats are Pacific Tracker (1965) and Pacific Collector (1970). The same story said the NSMV program turned Philly from a dormant yard with a few dozen workers into one employing more than 2,000 production workers. It labels TOTE's role vessel construction manager (VCM) rather than prime contractor, and adds that the approach cuts acquisition costs by at least 50%. The build years and the delivery target line up, so the premise holds, but the article explains the middleman as a cost model rather than a legal requirement.

Why is TOTE in the middle?

The twist is the structure. TOTE Services, a family-owned outfit run by a 27-year Coast Guard veteran, is the prime contractor managing the program, while Hanwha's Philly yard actually builds the hulls. Why the middleman? A US warship must be built in a US yard under US control (the Byrnes-Tollefson amendment). Philly clears that since it sits on US soil, but Hanwha doesn't yet hold the facility clearance (FCL) to be prime on a Navy job. Defense News pinned the contract at $2 billion and put first delivery in June 2030. That story headlines the $2 billion as awarded to Hanwha Philly Shipyard, which counts the deal differently from the prime-versus-sub split described here. Follow where the work goes and the two accounts agree. Follow who signs the contract and they part.

How far can Hanwha climb?

Meru notes Philly is now hiring a facility security officer, and Hanwha is weighing a $5B upgrade to turn the commercial yard into a real warship builder. The bigger idea: build hulls in Korea, arm them in the US. That split could help clear the US Navy's shipbuilding backlog, though it needs new law. Meru's one-liner: start as a subcontractor, bank the experience, climb to prime, the $100M Philly bet is starting to earn its keep.

Why it matters · US defense shipbuilding is a supply chain you enter once and keep selling into. This is Korea's foot in the door and a fresh growth leg for Hanwha's defense story.
