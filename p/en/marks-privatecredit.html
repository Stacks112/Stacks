AI made code cheap and private credit shook. Is this a real credit event, or just nerves?

Open in the Stacks app → Read the original ↗

Howard Marks has weighed in on the first big wobble in private credit. His conclusion up front: this episode is sentiment and fund flows, not actual defaults. But he sees the shape of it matching the anatomy of a bubble he has written about for decades. One thing to set straight first. The line "AI's first credit event" is a label the market and press attached, not Marks's own. He draws the opposite line, calling it flow and sentiment driven rather than credit deterioration.

What is private credit, and why did it shake?

Private credit, or direct lending, is when funds lend straight to companies instead of banks or the bond market. It is a lightly disclosed, hard-to-see market, and it has swelled from about 150 billion dollars two decades ago to roughly 2 trillion today. Software loans loomed especially large inside it. By Marks's figures, software is 20 to 30 percent of direct lending, versus just 4 to 5 percent of high-yield bonds and 10 to 15 percent of syndicated loans. The logic was that steady subscription revenue keeps these firms from failing. They were bought at around 20 times earnings, with heavy leverage layered on.

Why would AI shake a loan?

Lay the bridge first. The premise of these loans was that a software company's position is hard to take away. Then in November 2025 Anthropic shipped a powerful coding model, and by late January a wave of plug-ins followed, and the cost of writing code dropped. The moat that held the position started to give. In early February 2026 investors all reached for the same question at once. Are these companies still safe?

So did money actually get lost?

It did not. This is where the surface and the substance part ways.

Actual defaults sit near multi-year lows, which fits Marks's read that this is nerves, not lost money. But the share of loans trading cheap is rising, so the market is repricing fear ahead of any real default.

Checked 2026-07-10 · PitchBook LCD (Morningstar LSTA Leveraged Loan Index)

What happened in February was a rush for the exits, not a default. As redemption requests piled into semi-liquid funds, managers triggered the withdrawal limits built in, the redemption gates. The gates worked as designed and headed off a fire sale. Apollo capped withdrawals at 5 percent after requests reached 11 percent. A loop set in where blocking exits made people more nervous, but Marks reads this as a flow problem, not a solvency one. He does add one caveat: it is worth asking whether the NAVs people exited at, the values the funds set themselves, were overstated.

So what is Marks actually worried about?

His real warning sits elsewhere.

The common reading

AI torched the software moats. Software debt is the next domino.

VS
Marks's reading

The firms still pay their loans. This is sentiment, not defaults.

He points out that this stretch matches the anatomy of a bubble he has written about for decades. Something new appears, it holds a grain of truth, early winners draw envy, possibility hardens into certainty, and low-standard latecomers push prices up. The real problem is not one software loan. It is that the industry took in too much money and put it to work too fast, at standards that were too low. He points to the First Brands and Tricolor bankruptcies as the warning. He closes with Buffett. What the wise man does in the beginning, the fool does in the end.

In three lines
  • A Howard Marks memo, read across Wall Street. The software loans private funds long treated as safest have wobbled.
  • As AI cut the cost of writing code, doubt spread about whether these firms still have moats, and in February 2026 redemption requests piled up and funds gated withdrawals.
  • Marks's read is calm: this is sentiment and fund flows, not actual defaults. But he notes the shape matches the bubble anatomy he has written about for decades.

Why it matters · This is the first big memo treating AI not as a stock story but as a credit event. If AI can rewrite what a company is worth, it rewrites what its debt is worth.

Worth asking · If AI is rewriting company moats, should the debt lent to those companies be repriced too? Is this just nerves as Marks says, or the front edge of a bubble?
