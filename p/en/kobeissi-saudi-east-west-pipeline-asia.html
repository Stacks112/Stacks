YANBU ROUTE
The Kobeissi Letter (@KobeissiLetter) · 2026-07-27 · original: EN

South Korea loads 645,000 barrels a day at this Saudi Red Sea port. So why is the detour shrinking?

Open in the Stacks app → Read the original ↗

Since the Strait of Hormuz closed, Saudi crude bound for Asia crosses the country and leaves from the other coast. Taking May alone, China loaded about 774,000 barrels a day at that port, South Korea about 645,000, Japan about 452,000 and India about 387,000. Together the four take about 2.26 million barrels a day, more than half of everything leaving Yanbu.

Original post: The Kobeissi Letter (X, 2026-07-27) ↗

Where is Yanbu, and why there

The East-West pipeline runs 746 miles, about 1,200 kilometres, from the eastern oil fields to the Red Sea port of Yanbu. Crude that leaves from there never enters the Gulf, so it never has to pass through Hormuz. For South Korea, that means much of its Saudi crude is now loaded on the opposite sea from where it used to come. Loadings at Yanbu really did jump. They have run above 4 million barrels a day since June, against 973,000 barrels a day around the same period a year earlier. In the same report, industry sources say Yanbu has been fixing at maximum capacity for weeks and there is not much room for higher shipments.

So why are the volumes falling

This is where the numbers part ways. Wood Mackenzie's count has the Red Sea bypass peaking at about 4.07 million barrels a day in March and falling 41% to about 2.39 million by June. The same data has total Gulf crude exports collapsing 82%, from 18.8 million barrels a day in January to 3.4 million in June.

At the moment this route was described as having become critical, its volumes had been shrinking for four months. A route mattering more and a route carrying more are two different statements.

VS
Wood Mackenzie's reading

Volumes routed to the Red Sea fell from 4.07 million in March to 2.39 million in June. The detour is already retreating.

One side counts a single month's buyer list, the other counts four months of flow. That is why the same port points in two directions.

Does the detour have a chokepoint of its own

Before it closed, Hormuz carried about 11.4 million barrels a day. Even at its peak, the Yanbu route moved a little over a third of that. One pipeline does not stand in for one strait. Wood Mackenzie's Ian Solis puts it plainly: Yanbu has its own chokepoint. If Bab al-Mandeb comes under sustained disruption from a declared Houthi naval blockade, Asia stands to lose a major crude supply artery. Crude loaded at Yanbu still has to pass through that narrow entrance to reach Asia.

What settles it

The question comes down to one thing. Is the Yanbu route a replacement for Hormuz, or a buffer that buys time while costs climb? The place to watch is Bab al-Mandeb. If traffic through that strait holds, Yanbu looks like a replacement; if the Houthi blockade keeps turning ships around, it ends as a buffer. For South Korea this is not somebody else's problem. On May figures it was the second-largest buyer at that port, and the crude still arrives through one narrow entrance.

In three lines
  • With Hormuz closed, Saudi crude crosses the country by pipeline to the Red Sea port of Yanbu and loads there. In May, China, South Korea, Japan and India took 2.26 million barrels a day from that one port.
  • On the surface the detour has settled in: Yanbu loadings have gone from 973,000 barrels a day a year ago to above 4 million.
  • But Wood Mackenzie counts the Red Sea bypass falling 41%, from 4.07 million barrels a day in March to 2.39 million in June. And ships leaving Yanbu still have to pass the strait the Houthis declared blockaded.
