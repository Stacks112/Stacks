A week after Samsung paid its way out of one patent house, Micron walked into the same commission

Open in the Stacks app → Read the original ↗

On August 12 the US International Trade Commission opened a new investigation into Samsung Electronics and Samsung Electronics America. It was instituted on a complaint from Japan's Maxell and carries the docket number 337-TA-1516.

USITC Institutes Section 337 Investigation of Certain Mobile Electronic Devices (US International Trade Commission, 2026-08-12) ↗

The commission's notice records July 24 as the date Maxell filed its amended complaint, and the relief sought is a limited exclusion order together with cease and desist orders. The same day, a second complaint arrived at the same commission. This one came from Netlist, and it names Micron, Supermicro, HPE and Lenovo. Netlist is the company that closed a settlement with Samsung on August 5.

Why the border and not a fine

Section 337 sits in the Tariff Act of 1930. Its purpose is to keep infringing goods from entering the country and harming domestic industry, so this commission does not award damages.

Exclusion orderCustoms stops the goods at the border, and inventory already inside the country is frozen by a cease and desist order

A court collects money from the losing side. At the end of this path there is nothing left to sell. That is why companies that make their living from patents reach for this commission before they reach for a court.

Put simply, a court makes you pay after the crash. The commission keeps the car from crossing the border at all.

The month the customers became respondents

Netlist is a memory module company in Irvine, California. It holds patents that sit on server memory and HBM standards, and it has earned more from defending those patents than from selling product.

A settlement Samsung had resisted for three years after the verdict arrived one month after its customers appeared on the respondent list.

On the verdict alone Samsung had no reason to hurry. While the appeal bought time, the commission case moved forward, and on that docket sat the companies that sell products with Samsung HBM inside them. Once Google's TPUs and Supermicro's GPU servers can be stopped at customs, the counterparty in the negotiation is no longer Samsung alone.

The seat Micron now occupies

The complaint Netlist filed on August 12 walks that same sequence from the first step. The accused products are DDR5 RDIMMs and MRDIMMs, and four patents are asserted. The respondents are not only Micron but Supermicro, HPE and Lenovo, the companies that buy those modules and slot them into servers.

Netlist's release says its major competitors have licensed the patents for years while Micron continues to use them without authorisation. With Samsung and SK hynix each having settled their way out, that sentence points at the one company left. What remains on the Samsung side is the Maxell case. It is confined to phones and tablets, and feature patents covering cameras or unlocking are easier to design around than a memory standard. Whether that investigation ever reaches the customs desk, or simply sets the price of a settlement, is a different question from the one Netlist is asking. What separates the two cases is not the number of patents but the list of respondents. Maxell has two names on it, both of them Samsung. Netlist put the buyers on its list from the first day. With no customers on the list, delay favours the defendant. With customers on it, delay turns into cost.

In three lines
  • On August 12 the US International Trade Commission opened a Section 337 investigation of Samsung on a complaint from Japan's Maxell.
  • On the surface it reads as one more patent suit landing on Samsung.
  • But a second complaint reached the same commission that same day, and its target is Micron, filed by Netlist, the company that had settled with Samsung a week earlier.
