CXMT Jumped 466% on Debut to $487 Billion. Why Does Morningstar Call It 68% Overpriced?

Open in the Stacks app → Read the original ↗

Chinese memory chipmaker CXMT listed on Shanghai's STAR Market on July 27 and surged 466% on debut. Its closing market cap hit roughly 3.3 trillion yuan, about $487 billion, the highest of any mainland China-listed company. The IPO priced at 8.66 yuan a share and raised at least $8.6 billion.

Where does CXMT actually come from

Per Meru, the story starts with the 2018 US-China trade war, when Xi Jinping began personally pushing chip self-sufficiency, and Hefei, the capital of Anhui province, took the lead building CXMT into China's memory champion.

Meru argues Hefei was running a playbook it had already proven: in 2002, when Hyundai Electronics (now SK Hynix) sold off its LCD unit Hydis, buyer BOE got roughly $2.2 billion from Hefei and grew into today's largest LCD maker. In 2016, Hefei funded most of CXMT's $2.7 billion first-phase project the same way.

Is CXMT really a private company

Officially, CXMT has no single controlling shareholder, a framing independent sources confirm too. But Meru notes its top three shareholders are all Hefei-linked entities or the state semiconductor fund, and even after this IPO's dilution, their combined stake stays around 38%.

Where did the technology come from

CXMT's licensed DRAM patents trace to Qimonda, the German memory maker that went bankrupt in 2009. Qimonda was created in 2006 when Infineon, itself spun off from Siemens in 1999, split off its memory business, keeping a 77.5% stake from the start. After the bankruptcy, Infineon reacquired Qimonda's full patent estate in a 2014 settlement, and WiLAN's Irish subsidiary Polaris Innovations bought over 7,000 of those patents from Infineon in 2015 for about 30 million euros. CXMT signed a licensing deal with Polaris in December 2019.

WiLAN Acquires Qimonda Patent Portfolio from Infineon (GlobeNewswire, 2015-06-02) ↗

That release confirms the patents didn't pass directly from Qimonda's bankruptcy estate; Infineon bought them back first and sold them to Polaris afterward.

Analysts are split on the same closing price

Nomura's Donnie Teng set a price target of 116 yuan, about 13 times the IPO price and roughly 2.4 times the first-day close, betting AI-driven hyperscaler capex sustains memory demand and CXMT's share grows from 8% to 18% by 2028. Morningstar's Wei Jingjie takes the opposite view, pricing fair value at 14.90 yuan, 72% above the IPO price but 68% below the first-day close, arguing that without EUV lithography, CXMT's DUV-based process leaves its cost per bit over 30% above Samsung and SK Hynix.

What's the US wildcard here

Per CNBC, export controls on chipmaking tools remain CXMT's core challenge, and it may need at least 15% global share to stay competitive long-term. Some US lawmakers separately want to block American purchases of CXMT chips.

So what actually settles this

It comes down to one question: can CXMT close its cost-per-bit gap without EUV access, or does that gap persist and undercut today's valuation. If export controls hold and CXMT's share still climbs toward 15%, Nomura's case holds up. If the cost gap doesn't close and the price cycle cools, Morningstar's case holds up. Either way, day one's 466% is the start of the question, not the answer to it.

In three lines
  • Chinese DRAM maker CXMT surged 466% on its trading debut, becoming mainland China's most valuable listed company.
  • Meru argues this is the same state-driven chip playbook Hefei already proved with BOE.
  • But looking at the same closing price, Nomura sees another 2.4x upside while Morningstar sees it 68% too high.
