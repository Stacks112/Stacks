The same share cost 51% more in New York than in Seoul. Why didn't the gap close?

Open in the Stacks app → Read the original ↗

What an ADR actually is

Leave something with a warehouse and you get a receipt. Bring the receipt back and you get the thing. An ADR is that receipt, for shares. It lets a Korean stock trade in the United States. A bank holds the real shares in Seoul and issues matching receipts in New York. Being a receipt, it should be worth what the original is worth. SK hynix listed on Nasdaq on 10 July 2026 under the ticker SKHY. Citibank is the depositary, and one Seoul share equals ten US receipts. The company sold 177.9 million receipts at $149 for $26.5 billion, the largest US listing ever by a foreign company.

Can the receipt cost more than the thing?

It did, which is not supposed to happen.

The gap widens and narrows but does not converge on zero. Working arbitrage would have erased a spread that size within days. That points at the plumbing, not the price.

The US receipts jumped 27% in a single session on 14 July.

Why didn't arbitrage work?

Here is how it should run. Sell the expensive receipt in New York, buy the cheap share in Seoul, hand that share to the bank, get a new receipt, and deliver it. That round trip is what pins the two prices together. The last step was blocked. Newly issued Seoul shares cannot be transferred until they are listed on the Korea Exchange. So Citibank kept the books for issuing and cancelling receipts closed until 29 July. One explanation worth correcting while we are here. People have said the cause is a Korean short selling ban. That is not right. Short selling resumed on all listed stocks on 31 March 2025 and works normally today. What was shut is not short selling. It is the channel between the two markets.

Does it close after the 29th?

The channel opens, but it is narrow. SK hynix can convert only 2.5% of its shares outstanding into US receipts, and the $26.5 billion offering already consumed that entire allowance. The company could have used up to 25%. It used 2.5%.

The common reading

The channel opens on 29 July, so the premium goes away shortly after.

VS
What the structure says

Only one direction really opens. With no headroom to create new receipts, US supply does not grow.

Has this happened before?

It has. India ran the same plumbing. Across eleven Indian ADRs the average premium was 17% over the five years from 2001 to 2005, with individual names reaching 50%. As of July 2005, Infosys stood at 39% and State Bank of India at 28%. The cause was identical. Converting an ADR into shares was free, but converting shares into ADRs was allowed only up to the amount previously converted the other way. Once that headroom filled, the premium stayed for years.

Premiums in the Indian ADR Market: An Analysis of Trends and Causes (NYU Stern, 2006) ↗

The premium is a live gauge of how much foreign money wants Korean memory. It is also a sign that the money cannot get in through Seoul.

In three lines
  • The same SK hynix share briefly cost 51% more on Nasdaq than in Seoul.
  • By the textbook, arbitrage should close that in days: sell the expensive one, buy the cheap one. But the channel between the two markets was shut until July 29.
  • Even open, the channel is narrow. Only 2.5% of shares outstanding can be converted into US receipts, and this offering used all of it.

Why it matters · The premium gauges how badly foreign money wants Korean memory, and shows that the money cannot get in through Seoul. What holds the gap open is not sentiment. It is a 2.5% conversion cap.

Worth asking · What holds the gap open is a conversion cap, not demand. Given the same company, would you buy the expensive side with a blocked channel, or the cheap side with no channel at all?
