A rate cut will not pull the 30-year down with it

Open in the Stacks app → Read the original ↗

The 30-year US Treasury yield has climbed above 5.2%, its highest level since 2007. Long-term yields are the reference point that mortgages, corporate bonds and the government's own interest bill all sit on top of, so when this number rises, the cost of borrowing goes up for everyone from a family buying a house to the US Treasury itself.

The price set in the secondary market and the price actually paid at the August 13 auction are almost the same. The market granted no discount, which means the price of finding someone to absorb long-dated paper has hardened at this level.

The article reads the same auction from the fiscal side. The deficit accumulated so far this fiscal year is $1.17 trillion, 15% larger than a year earlier, and the price of finding buyers for the debt that funds it is the highest in 25 years.

When a cut and a runoff happen together

The answer the market is waiting for is a rate cut. But what the policy rate moves directly is the short end. The price of money you get back in 30 years is set by inflation over those 30 years and by how much paper the government prints in the meantime. When the Fed shrinks its balance sheet, the long-dated bonds sitting in its vault flow back into the market. The buyers stay the same while the supply grows, so the price falls, and a falling bond price is the same sentence as a rising yield.

The common reading

If the Fed cuts, borrowing costs come down across the board, so the 30-year eventually follows.

VS
Meru's reading

A cut lowers short rates while balance-sheet runoff pushes long bonds back onto the market. Do both and only the gap between short and long widens.

Readers split on how far the effect of the same cut travels, and what decides the split is not the size of the cut but the balance sheet.

The resignation Warsh handed in during 2011

Only one tool pulls long yields down directly: quantitative easing, in which the Fed prints dollars and buys long-dated Treasuries. The Treasury's own buyback swaps stale long bonds for freshly issued bills, so no new money enters the system. Quantitative easing brings in money that did not exist and removes the long bonds outright. That single difference separates the two tools. The problem is who holds that tool now. Kevin Warsh stepped down as a Fed governor in March 2011, and the move is said to have followed his doubts about the second round of quantitative easing pushed by Ben Bernanke. In 2014 he called quantitative easing a reverse Robin Hood policy that fed the market. He was confirmed by the Senate 54 to 45 on May 13, 2026, and took the chair on May 22.

Kevin Warsh comes into the Fed facing a big 'family fight' over cutting interest rates (CNBC, 2026-05-16)
출처: cnbc.com

The piece reports that even before taking office Warsh sat on the side of cutting rates while continuing to shrink the balance sheet, and that the combination was contested inside the Fed. This is the fuse Meru points at. The cut is what Trump wants and the runoff is what Warsh wants, and doing both at once can send the 30-year the other way.

The Senate vote scheduled for September

If the Fed will not move, where is the hand that can hold long yields down. The remaining route is to issue fewer long bonds, and that requires someone to buy bills instead. The CLARITY Act, waiting for another Senate vote in September, stands in that spot. If the bill that sets the rules for crypto markets passes, the regulatory fog over dollar-pegged stablecoins lifts, and issuers hold most of their reserves in short-term US Treasuries. Fed Governor Stephen Miran said in a November 2025 speech that the interquartile range of private estimates compiled by Fed staff puts stablecoin uptake at $1 trillion to $3 trillion by the end of the decade. If that much demand for bills appears, the Treasury can issue more short and less long, holding long yields down by going around them. If the Senate reaches 60 votes in September, one more hand joins the effort, and if it falls short again what is left is the slow road of waiting for inflation to come down on its own. So to read the direction of the 30-year now, watching the Fed chair's convictions and the Senate's vote count works faster than watching what else the Treasury secretary pulls out.

In three lines
  • The 30-year US Treasury yield is above 5.2%, the highest since 2007, and the 10-year sits near 4.7%.
  • On the surface the answer is a rate cut, and both Trump and the market are waiting for the Fed to deliver one.
  • But a cut lowers short rates. Shrink the balance sheet at the same time and the long bonds in the Fed's vault return to the market, pushing the 30-year the other way.
