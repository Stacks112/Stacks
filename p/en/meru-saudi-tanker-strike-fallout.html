Houthis strike two Saudi-owned tankers by name, following through on a five-day-old blockade warning

Open in the Stacks app → Read the original ↗

Yemen's Houthi forces claimed strikes on two Saudi-linked oil tankers, the Korean investment blogger Meru writes, days after warning global shipping firms that any vessel touching Saudi ports would be a target.

What's confirmed and what isn't

Houthi spokesman Yahya Saree claimed the July 22 strikes on the product tanker Encelia and the VLCC Layla. The Encelia incident is the better-corroborated of the two, with a UKMTO (United Kingdom Maritime Trade Operations) notification and a visible fire reported. The Layla strike is Houthi-claimed but has not been independently confirmed by outside reporting. Bahri, Saudi Arabia's state shipping line majority owned by the sovereign wealth fund and Aramco, is confirmed as Layla's owner; Encelia's ownership was not independently verified in available reporting.

The warning came first

On July 20, the Houthis told global shipping firms by email that they would treat any vessel loading or unloading cargo at Saudi ports as fair game, regardless of flag. Foreign tankers are not waiting to find out if the threat extends to them. The Houthis themselves claimed nearly ten ships have turned back, a figure that could not be independently corroborated through a specific vessel count as this card originally suggested. EU Naval Force Aspides has advised vessels linked to Israeli, US, or Saudi interests to avoid the Red Sea and Gulf of Aden until threat levels ease.

A geography that has frustrated past US strikes

Meru points out the Houthis hold a geographic advantage that has frustrated past US military action: mountainous terrain riddled with tunnels and bunkers that a 52-day American bombing campaign in 2025, Operation Rough Rider, could not fully destroy despite striking more than 1,000 targets over March 15 to May 6.

The quieter cost is already showing up

War-risk insurance premiums on Red Sea and Gulf of Aden voyages have already been rising since the attacks, and shipping-trade coverage points to Red Sea transit volumes falling to multi-month lows. That is Meru's core point: the delivered cost of Middle East crude climbs quietly through insurance and rerouting well before any single blockade escalation makes headlines of its own.

In three lines
  • Houthi forces claimed strikes on two Saudi-linked tankers, the product tanker Encelia and VLCC Layla, days after warning any vessel touching Saudi ports would be a target.
  • The Encelia strike is corroborated by a UKMTO notification and visible fire; the Layla strike is Houthi-claimed but not independently confirmed, and only Layla's Bahri ownership is verified.
  • A Reuters count of '7 vessels reversing course' in the card's original source could not be verified; the Houthis themselves claimed 'nearly ten' ships turned back.
