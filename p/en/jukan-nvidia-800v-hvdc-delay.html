SemiAnalysis says Nvidia's next power system slips a year. Supply chain sources call it a validation delay, not a roadmap change

July 23, 2026

Nvidia's next-generation 800 volt HVDC power system, a way of delivering electricity inside AI server racks that cuts energy loss and lets racks scale to higher power levels, may not reach large-scale production until 2028 or later, a year later than the 2027 timeline the market had expected, according to research firm SemiAnalysis.

Why power, not chip speed, is the bottleneck now

HVDC, short for high-voltage direct current, replaces the many small power converters inside a server rack with fewer, more efficient ones. That matters because AI racks are increasingly limited by how much power and cooling they can pack in, not by chip performance.

Two different reads on the same delay

SemiAnalysis's own reporting has pointed to hyperscalers favoring a more mature low-voltage transition over immediate 800V adoption, alongside related delays in co-packaged optics for Nvidia's Rubin Ultra platform, as part of the story. Semiconductor analyst Jukan, citing supply chain sources, argues the market has misread the delay differently: the issue is a validation problem with first-generation product designs, not a change to Nvidia's technology roadmap. Power cabinets, battery backup units, and battery packs have all been resubmitted for validation testing, these sources say, a normal part of qualifying new hardware.

SemiAnalysis's read

Hyperscalers are holding back on 800V adoption itself, alongside related packaging delays, pushing the real ramp to 2028

VS
Jukan's supply-chain sources

The roadmap hasn't changed. First-gen hardware just needs to be revalidated, and volume production is still coming in 1Q 2027

Delta Electronics is said to be ready

Taiwan's Delta Electronics, a major supplier of data center power systems, battery backup units, and energy-management platforms for this architecture, is described as positioned for a ramp once validation clears.

What's actually riding on this

The system is meant to support Nvidia's upcoming Vera Rubin chip platform, and any slip in power hardware readiness could delay how fast data centers can deploy Rubin at scale, even if the chips themselves ship on time. Whether the real culprit is hyperscaler caution or first-gen validation delays matters less to data center operators than the bottom line: how much power they can actually plug in, and when.
