COUNTRY RISK
Aswath Damodaran (Musings on Markets) · 2026-07-15 · original: EN

The same profit is worth less depending on where it's earned. How does Damodaran price that?

Open in the Stacks app → Read the original ↗

Damodaran's conclusion boils down to one line. A company's risk is set by where it earns money, not where it is listed. His annual country-risk update, the 2026 edition, is posted free and in full on his blog.

But isn't country risk diversifiable?

The old textbook taught that country risk is something you can erase by spreading investments across the world. Damodaran thinks that was wrong then and is more wrong now. Markets move together in every crisis, so spreading the bet does not wash the risk out. On top of that, a company's revenue and costs increasingly come from many countries. So the risk attaches to the map it does business on, not to its headquarters address.

How does risk change the price?

Start with the term. A discount rate is the interest-like rate used to pull future earnings back to today's value. Earn in a riskier country and that discount rate rises. The same expected profit is then worth less today. The equity risk premium, or ERP, is the extra return investors demand for holding stocks instead of a safe government bond. The higher the country risk, the larger this premium, and the more the value is cut.

How do you measure that premium?

2026 mature-market ERP4.20%
US implied ERP (S&P 500)4.42%
Relative-risk multiplier1.55x

Damodaran starts from the 4.42% ERP implied by US stock prices, strips out the default piece of US credit to reach a 4.20% mature-market floor, then for each country scales its sovereign default spread up by an equity-volatility multiplier of 1.55. The value here is the method, not the number.

So the safe mature market starts at 4.2 percent, and the riskier the country, the more gets added on top, lifting the discount rate. He covers about 180 countries this year. In his data, places like India and Brazil carry premiums a few points above the US. The exact figures sit in the table he publishes.

What if even the US isn't risk-free?

It is worth chewing on that he no longer treats even the US as fully riskless. He rates it Aa1, not the top grade.

Moody's strips U.S. of top credit rating over failure to rein in debt (PBS News, 2025-05-16)
출처: pbs.org

He is not alone in that call. Moody's cut the US from Aaa to Aa1 in May 2025, projecting federal deficits widening from 6.4% of GDP in 2024 to nearly 9% by 2035. The benchmark everyone used for "risk-free" has started to carry a price of its own.

So what does an investor take away?

This is not a call to buy or sell any particular stock.

The old textbook's reading

Country risk washes out if you diversify, so ignore it.

VS
Damodaran's reading

Where a firm operates changes its risk, and what you should pay.

The split is over what country risk is: a cost you can diversify away, or a property that has to be priced in. The takeaway is a durable framework. When valuing a business exposed to emerging markets or geopolitical risk, it turns that risk from a vague unease into a number in the discount rate that changes the price.
