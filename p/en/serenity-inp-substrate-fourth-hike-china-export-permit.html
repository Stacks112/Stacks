The colors an optical fiber likes

At each end of the cable running between data center racks sits a part about the size of a finger. Inside that part, called an optical transceiver, an electrical signal becomes light. The laser that actually produces that light is grown on a base of indium phosphide. Optical fiber does not carry every color of light equally well. At 1310 nanometers the signal does not spread, and at 1550 nanometers the least of it leaks away. Long-distance optical links therefore use only these two wavelengths. This is why silicon cannot take over the job. Silicon converts electricity into light so inefficiently that it never becomes a laser, and gallium arsenide does emit light but in the 850 to 980 nanometer range, which is not a color the fiber likes. Put simply, indium phosphide is close to the only material that can produce the colors the fiber will carry. It is also why the spread of silicon photonics increases demand for indium phosphide rather than reducing it. The path the light travels can be etched in silicon, but the place where the light is made still needs a separate indium phosphide chip attached.

Lumentum CEO warns of impending bottleneck on critical material used for silicon photonics (Tom's Hardware, 2026-07-31)
출처: tomshardware.com

Michael Hurlston, chief executive of the optical component maker Lumentum, said supply currently falls more than 30 percent short of customer demand. He said the shortage would become worse than the one in memory. Lumentum's revenue in the same quarter was $808.4 million, up 90 percent from a year earlier.

A different set of companies takes the substrate and grows a thin crystal layer on top of it. The process is called epitaxy and the product an epitaxial wafer. The Taiwanese firms the post named as beneficiaries, Visual Photonics Epitaxy and Landmark Optoelectronics, sit at this step. When substrate prices rise, the price of these wafers follows. They have already risen twice, and a third increase is reported to be under discussion for the fourth quarter.

There is a second hand on the price

The post reads the increase as demand outrunning supply. But this material has a second hand on its price that moves regardless of demand. AXT, second by share, runs its plant in Beijing. Its subsidiary Beijing Tongmei needs an export license from China's commerce ministry for each shipment of indium phosphide substrate. Licenses came slowly in the fourth quarter of 2025 and revenue was held down; they widened during 2026 and revenue jumped. First-quarter revenue was $26.9 million, of which indium phosphide was $13.6 million. Second-quarter revenue rose to $47.6 million with net income of $11.1 million. The raw material points the same way. About 70 percent of refined indium output sits in China, and the spot price reached $500 to $600 per kilogram in February 2026, the highest since 2015.

Where AXT's third quarter decides it

So does this price increase reach the seller's income statement, or does it stop at a quoted price with material that cannot ship? The same quarterly report separates the two. If AXT's third-quarter revenue exceeds the second quarter, the increase actually reached the invoice. If it does not, the price rose while export licenses or capacity held the volume back. With this material, raising the price and collecting it are not the same event.

In three lines
  • The price of a material used in AI data center optics has now risen for four quarters running.
  • Only three companies make it and a new plant takes years, so it reads as demand outrunning supply.
  • But there is a second hand on the price. AXT, second by share, runs its plant in Beijing and needs a Chinese export license for every shipment.
