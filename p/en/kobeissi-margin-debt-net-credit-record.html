BREAKING: Net credit balances fell -$70 billion in June, to a record -$1.06 trillion.

This metric tracks how much margin debt investors carry relative to the cash in their brokerage accounts.

Since the 2022 bear market, this figure has more than quadrupled.

This comes as…

July 25, 2026

A fresh sign of investors piling into stocks with borrowed money rather than cash has emerged. According to The Kobeissi Letter, net credit balances (the cash investors hold in brokerage accounts minus margin debt) fell $70 billion in June to a record negative $1.06 trillion. Margin debt itself climbed $895 billion over the same period to a record $1.50 trillion.

Checking the numbers first

FINRA's own raw data confirms these figures almost exactly. June 2026 margin debt came to $1.502 trillion, and combined free credit balances across cash and margin accounts came to $440.9 billion. Subtract one from the other and you get negative $1.061 trillion, matching Kobeissi's figure closely.

But 'broad risk appetite' may only be half the story

Kobeissi contrasts this with 2008, when investors held more cash and net credit balances were positive. Other sources, though, put both the 2000 dot-com peak (-$130 billion) and the 2008 peak (-$60 billion) in negative territory, suggesting this comparison may be mixing different measurement methodologies. That discrepancy isn't fully resolved as of this writing. The stronger evidence is about where the leverage is actually concentrated. Per Crypto Briefing, leveraged ETFs hold about $179 billion in total assets, and 85% of that sits in just three sectors: tech, AI and semiconductors. On June 5, a 3x leveraged semiconductor ETF fell 31% in a single session. In July, South Korea's Kospi dropped 27% after SK hynix said it would slow AI memory expansion, triggering margin calls across roughly 1.2 million Korean brokerage accounts, about 10% of all trading accounts in the country, that had bet on the sector through leveraged ETFs.

What other sources suggest

The leverage isn't spread broadly, it's concentrated in AI and semiconductor leveraged ETFs and derivatives, and that structure has already blown up once, in Korea.

"Investors love risk across the board" and "leverage is stacked in one AI-shaped corner of the market" paint very different pictures of what happens in the next pullback.

Has margin debt historically warned of corrections

Three prior episodes of margin debt surging more than 65%, March 2000 (dot-com), July 2007 (financial crisis) and October 2021 (2022 bear market), all preceded market tops by roughly two to six months. This is being counted as a fourth instance of the pattern. The sample size is only three or four cycles, though, and some analysts note debt and markets tend to rise together, making this more of a coincident indicator than a precise timing tool.

So what's the thing to actually watch

The question comes down to one thing: is a record-low net credit balance a warning for the whole market, or a risk concentrated in one AI/semiconductor-shaped corner of it. If cracks show up first in sector-specific leveraged products, as they did in Korea, it's the latter. If a pullback spreads across the broader market, the former reading was right. Margin debt doesn't cause a decline, but once one starts, it can make it bigger.

  • US net credit balances hit a record negative $1.06 trillion in June, margin debt a record $1.5 trillion.
  • Checking FINRA's own raw data, these figures hold up almost exactly.
  • But rather than broad risk appetite, there's substantial evidence this leverage is concentrated in AI and semiconductor leveraged ETFs.
