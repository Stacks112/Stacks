The 10-Year Treasury Yield Hit 4.7% and Oil Got the Blame. So Why Didn't Inflation Expectations Move?

Open in the Stacks app → Read the original ↗

The 10-year Treasury yield broke above 4.7% intraday. Meru argues oil alone can't explain this, pointing out that breakeven inflation has barely moved while the 30-year real yield jumped to its highest since the 2008 financial crisis.

Did breakeven inflation really stay flat

Checking Meru's core premise first, an Axios report from July 23 backs it up directly: the bond market's long-run inflation pricing has barely changed even as Treasury yields have climbed. The 10-year breakeven rate stayed between 2.20% and 2.28% all month. Meanwhile the 30-year real yield climbed to 2.95%, the highest since this metric started being tracked in 2010. Bloomberg reported on July 9 that a 30-year auction drew the highest yield since 2007. Meru's read, that this is about the cost of money itself rather than inflation, holds up against the data.

Higher Treasury yields signal new era for interest rates (Axios, 2026-07-23) ↗

But is the IEEPA refund figure accurate

No. The card's claim of $180 billion a year through 2036 isn't accurate. The actual Penn Wharton Budget Model estimate from February 20, 2026 puts the refund liability from the IEEPA tariff ruling at $175-182 billion, a one-time total, not a recurring annual figure. CNBC and Reuters independently reported the same $175 billion refund number. The through 2036 framing belongs to a different estimate. The Committee for a Responsible Federal Budget separately projected the ruling could cost $1.7-1.9 trillion in cumulative lost revenue through 2036, a $2.2-2.4 trillion debt impact with interest, which averages to roughly $150-190 billion a year. It looks like two different institutions' two different numbers got merged into one.

Supreme Court tariffs ruling could put U.S. on hook for $175 billion in refunds (CNBC, 2026-02-20) ↗

What about the rest of the argument

China selling Treasuries while buying gold, Japanese investors net-selling $29.6 billion in Q1 2026, and record global corporate bond issuance of $3.68 trillion in H1 2026 with Amazon's $54 billion deal, all check out independently through Nikkei Asia, CNBC and other outlets. One wrinkle: Amazon said after that bond sale it won't issue more debt in 2026 (CNBC, July 7), so whether Big Tech's bond rush keeps up the same pace is worth watching.

So what's the thing to actually watch

If this is about oil, yields should fall once the Strait of Hormuz reopens, a matter of weeks. If it's about supply and demand, it only eases once the deficit and corporate bond issuance slow down, a matter of years. One number needs correcting, but the core diagnosis, flat inflation expectations paired with rising real yields, holds up. The next thing to check is whether the Treasury's August 5 quarterly refunding announcement drops the language guaranteeing stable auction sizes, as Meru flagged.

In three lines
  • Meru argues the Treasury yield spike to 4.7% isn't mainly about oil, pointing instead to the deficit and Big Tech bond issuance crowding out Treasury demand.
  • One supporting figure claims IEEPA tariff refunds add $180 billion a year through 2036 to the deficit.
  • But the real Wharton estimate is a one-time $175-182 billion refund liability, not an annual figure, likely conflated with a separate CRFB estimate of $1.7-1.9 trillion in cumulative losses through 2036. The rest of the structural argument mostly checks out independently.

Why it matters · Meru's core diagnosis, that the yield spike is about the deficit and supply and demand rather than oil, holds up against the data, but the IEEPA refund figure conflates two different institutions' numbers into one. One wrong figure doesn't necessarily mean the broader diagnosis is wrong.

Worth asking · The IEEPA figure needs correcting, but the core data on flat inflation expectations and rising real yields still holds. Is that enough to still trust Meru's diagnosis that this is a structural supply and demand story rather than an oil story?
