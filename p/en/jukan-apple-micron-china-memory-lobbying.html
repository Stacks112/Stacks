Apple Wants Chinese Memory Chips, But Not Because They're Cheaper

Open in the Stacks app → Read the original ↗

Apple is lobbying the White House to allow Chinese-made memory chips in its products, according to the Wall Street Journal. The chips in question come from Chinese DRAM maker CXMT and NAND maker YMTC. Micron, the US memory chipmaker, is reportedly pushing back hard, arguing the move should never be allowed. Chip-industry commentator Jukan, writing on X, disputed Apple's reasoning.

This is a blacklist fight, not a tariff fight

The dispute is about export-control and national-security blacklists, not tariff exemptions. Two separate lists are in play: the Pentagon's "1260H list," which designates CXMT as a Chinese military-linked company, and Commerce's Entity List, which would effectively cut off US companies from doing business with CXMT if it's added. Apple is lobbying to keep CXMT off that list, not asking for a tariff discount.

'CXMT is cheap' is only half true

A July 26 comparison by Tom's Hardware found CXMT's 64GB DDR5 module priced about 2.2% below equivalent Samsung/SK hynix modules, despite a 40% larger die and worse yields. WCCFTech reported the same finding from Computex in June: "CXMT cheap is a myth, prices are almost on par with the big three." Apple's real motive for wanting CXMT appears to be supply, not price. While Samsung, SK hynix and Micron divert capacity toward AI-driven HBM, CXMT has no reason to make that pivot, leaving it with relatively more available inventory and flexible contract terms.

The '$250 price hike' isn't a settled fact yet either

Jukan argued memory prices jumped roughly fivefold while raising Apple's per-unit cost by only $50, even as Apple raised iPhone prices by $250. Checking this, Apple hasn't actually raised iPhone prices yet. The real price increases announced June 25 hit Macs, iPads and Vision Pro: MacBook Air up $200, MacBook Pro up $500, Vision Pro up $500. The $250 figure is an IDC-style projection for the unreleased iPhone 18 Pro (estimates range $100-270, mostly clustered around $200), not a hike that's already happened. Separately, iPhone 17 prices did rise in Japan, but Forbes attributed that to a weak yen, not memory costs. The "$50 memory cost versus $250 iPhone hike" comparison mixes something that's already happened with something projected, and two different causes.

The Micron-Apple grudge is real, though

One of Jukan's other claims holds up well. Reports sourced to the WSJ confirm Micron CEO Sanjay Mehrotra has a genuinely long-running tension with Apple, dating back to his time as a SanDisk co-founder in 1988, when he reportedly avoided meeting Apple representatives altogether. Mehrotra met directly with Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick last week to oppose Apple's request, offering Micron's $250 billion US manufacturing investment through 2035 (Idaho, New York, Virginia) as the alternative.

So what's the thing to actually watch

The question comes down to one thing: is Apple's lobbying really about protecting consumers from price increases, or cover for securing supply. If Apple wins approval for CXMT and YMTC and iPhone prices come down or hold steady, supply really was the goal. If prices go up anyway after approval, the "inflation protection" framing won't hold up.

In three lines
  • Apple is lobbying the White House to allow Chinese memory chips (CXMT, YMTC), per WSJ.
  • But CXMT's DDR5 pricing runs only about 2% below Samsung/SK hynix, undercutting the 'it's cheap' assumption.
  • The real reason is supply, not price, CXMT hasn't diverted capacity to HBM, so it has more available inventory.
