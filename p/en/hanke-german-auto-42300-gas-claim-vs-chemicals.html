Was it really Russian gas that erased 42,300 German auto jobs?

Germany’s auto industry has LOST 42,400 jobs in ONE year.

Employment in the auto industry is now at its LOWEST since 2005.

THE CUT OFF OF CHEAP RUSSIAN GAS AND GERMANY'S WAR AGAINST RUSSIA ARE TURNING GERMANY INTO AN INDUSTRIAL WASTELAND.

August 15, 2026

German carmakers and their suppliers shed 42,300 jobs in a single year. Employment stood at 691,500 at the end of June, the lowest level since 2005. That is a 5.8% drop.

German car industry sheds jobs at a record pace (dpa, 2026-08-14)
출처: finance.yahoo.com

The person who put that number back into circulation is Steve Hanke, a monetary economist. In the same post he named a single cause. Cheap Russian gas was cut off, and Germany is at war with Russia, and this is turning the country into an industrial wasteland. Naming a cause is the kind of claim you can check. If the price of gas is the culprit, the industries that burn the most gas should be the ones falling apart.

The industries that burn the most gas

In German manufacturing, the biggest gas consumer is chemicals and pharmaceuticals. Gas heats their plants, and it is also the raw material that becomes plastics and fertiliser. Car plants, by comparison, lean on electricity more than on gas. The accounting firm EY counted German industrial employment for the whole of 2025. The sector lost 124,000 jobs, and about 50,000 of them were in cars. Chemicals and pharmaceuticals lost about 2,000.

If gas were the cause, chemicals and pharmaceuticals, the heaviest gas users in the country, should have been hit hardest. The industry that actually shrank was cars. The cause Hanke names does not explain that gap.

In the same study EY listed its own reasons. German carmakers are moving production abroad, and industrial revenue has fallen almost 5% since 2023. Energy prices do not appear on that list as a standalone item.

The segment that added people

Break the statistical office's count into segments and the direction is not uniform. Parts and accessories makers were down 7.6%, and vehicle manufacturers were down 6.1%. Bodywork and trailer makers, though, were up 10%, to 42,800. All three pay the same gas price and the same electricity bill in the same country. Explain everything with energy and the third segment is left over. Put simply, this looks less like the whole plant losing power and more like specific lines losing orders. Bodywork and trailers ride commercial and specialty vehicle demand, while parts suppliers take the full force of production moving abroad and of Chinese competitors. When two groups pay the same fuel bill and one shrinks while the other grows, what separated them is the order book, not the meter.

Between 42,300 and 47,000

Hanke's 42,400 differs from the statistical office count by 100. That looks like rounding. The German automotive industry association published a different survey in February. It put 2025 employment in the sector at 726,000 and said the year had cost 47,000 jobs. The scope and the timing differ, so the numbers differ. Which one you quote moves the decline by nearly 5,000 people. What is at stake is when to look at European auto suppliers again. If energy costs are the cause, employment returns as gas prices come down. If the cause is relocation and competition, the decline continues regardless of gas. If the statistical office's next half-year count, due in August 2027, falls below this year's level again, the cause sits outside energy.

In three lines
  • German auto employment fell by 42,300 in a year, to 691,500, the lowest since 2005.
  • Hanke blames the cut-off of cheap Russian gas and Germany's stance toward Russia.
  • Yet chemicals and pharmaceuticals, the country's heaviest gas users, lost about 2,000 jobs last year while cars lost about 50,000.
