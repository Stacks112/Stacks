Higher rates don't shrink what tech companies will earn, they shrink what that money is worth today

Open in the Stacks app → Read the original ↗

The 30-year US Treasury yield has climbed to 5.3%, its highest level since 2007. On the same day CoreWeave fell 8.3% and Fabrinet fell 10%, while SK Hynix, SanDisk and Western Digital each dropped more than 5%.

Rising Yields, Oil Interrupt Rally, Hurting Chips (Charles Schwab, 2026-08-18)
출처: schwab.com

None of that means these companies will earn less money this year. What shrank is the rate used to convert that money into today's value.

Pushing 10 million won ten years out

The discount rate is the rate at which money you will receive later is marked down to what it is worth now. Put simply, it is an interest rate turned around and run backwards. Say you are due 10 million won a year from now. At a 4% rate you only need to put 9.61 million won on deposit today to end up with 10 million, so that future money is worth 9.61 million won today. At 5% you need only 9.52 million won. The same future sum just lost 90,000 won of present value. Stretch the wait to ten years and the gap changes shape. At 4% you need 6.76 million won today; at 5% you need 6.14 million. The same one percentage point now costs 620,000 won, close to seven times the one-year figure. Compounding does that: interest earns interest, and ten years of it piles up.

Where banks and tech part ways

A bank that pays a steady dividend hands you money that behaves like one-year money. The profits an AI company promises sit several years out. So the same one percentage point moves the two share prices by very different amounts. That is the first shock, and it arrives fast. Nothing about the company's earnings changed. Only the price tag attached to those earnings did.

When the spread is thin, the Treasury yield passes straight through

The second shock is slower. Companies borrow at the Treasury yield plus a spread that reflects their own credit risk. A wide spread absorbs part of a rise in Treasury yields. A thin one lets it through untouched. That is the author's premise, so we went and looked the numbers up.

30-year Treasury yield5.31%
10-year Treasury yield4.72%
10-year real yield2.44%
10-year breakeven inflation2.28%
Investment-grade corporate spread0.81%
High-yield corporate spread2.70%

What moved was the real yield, not breakeven inflation. Money itself got more expensive, and this is not an inflation scare. At 0.81% the investment-grade spread is far too thin to absorb the move in Treasury yields, which supports the author's premise. It is still wider than the 0.53% of October 1997, so it is not a record low.

When borrowing costs rise, the businesses hurt first are the ones that spend heavily up front and recover it later through usage fees, which is exactly how a data centre works. The rent has to rise as much as the funding cost did to break even, and when competition stops that from happening, the difference comes out of profit.

출처: thestreet.com

As that report notes, yields were not the only weight on the market that day. Oil rose too, and the two pushed in the same direction on the arithmetic of data-centre investment.

What Nvidia can answer on August 26

The rule that higher rates sink tech stocks does not always hold. Rates were high all through 2026, and tech went to record highs anyway. A discount rate is only the measuring stick. If earnings grow faster than the stick lengthens, the stick loses. So the question now is not the direction of yields but the speed of earnings. Nvidia reports its fiscal 2027 second quarter on August 26, and that number is the speedometer. Revenue and next-quarter guidance above expectations would be evidence that the sector can carry a heavier stick. Falling short would undercut the very reason the market has been willing to pay these prices at these yields.

In three lines
  • Borrowing money is the most expensive it has been since 2007, and AI-linked shares felt it first.
  • It looks as if higher rates shrink what tech companies will earn. They do not.
  • What shrinks is the rate that converts that money into today's value, and on ten-year money it bites close to seven times as hard as on one-year money.
