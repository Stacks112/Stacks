Lower-paid workers got the faster raise, but inflation is still there

Open in the Stacks app → Read the original ↗

Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent's claim has three layers: the bottom of the wage distribution rose faster than the top, median pay beat inflation, and the change proves that Trump administration policy is turning the economy around. The first two calculations match the Bureau of Labor Statistics tables. Read to the end, though, the same release shows a much smaller gain in purchasing power than in nominal pay and leaves gaps between one full-time-worker data point, all Americans, and a policy verdict.

The lower-end gain is real, but it is a cutoff, not an average

Put the second quarters of 2025 and 2026 side by side. The 25th-percentile boundary rose $44, from $806 to $850, or 5.5%. The median rose $55, from $1,196 to $1,251, or 4.6%. The 75th percentile rose $28, from $1,887 to $1,915, or 1.5%. Bessent's arithmetic that the lower boundary grew more than three times as fast as the upper one is correct.

Usual Weekly Earnings of Wage and Salary Workers, Q2 2025 (U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, 2025-07-22)
출처: bls.gov

The $850 figure is not the average pay of the bottom quarter. It is the boundary below which 25% of full-time wage and salary workers fall. Nor does that rate mean the same group of lower-paid workers received an average raise of that size.

It is not the same as wage growth for the same people

BLS itself warns that quantile boundaries can move when the composition of the workforce changes. A shift in the weight of a lower-paid age group, or a cluster of responses around one dollar value, can move the boundary even when individual wage paths are different. The person at the 25th percentile need not be the same person in both quarters. The Atlanta Fed's Wage Growth Tracker asks a different question. It matches the hourly wage of the same individual 12 months apart and takes the median change. In June 2026, its overall tracker was 3.6%, with 3.4% for job stayers and 4.1% for job switchers. That does not refute the BLS percentile result. One measure tracks where a cross-sectional boundary moved; the other tracks how pay changed for matched people.

After inflation, the $55 gain becomes $2

The nominal median for full-time workers rose $55 a week. In the same table, the inflation-adjusted median in 1982-84 dollars rose only $2, from $372 to $374. Nominal pay at 4.6% did beat CPI inflation at 3.9%, but the spread was 0.7 percentage point and the real increase shown in the table was about 0.5%.

There is another boundary around the headline. It covers 120.945 million wage and salary workers who usually work at least 35 hours a week. It excludes the self-employed and part-time workers. In Table 6 of the same release, median weekly earnings for part-time workers fell from $396 to $391. "Americans across the distribution" works inside the full-time table, not across the entire labor market.

The policy verdict gets another test on October 21

Bessent tied the result to President Trump turning the tide and used it to reject the K-shaped-economy narrative. The BLS table verifies an outcome, not its cause. It cannot separate tax policy from labor demand, industry mix, job switching, or labor supply. Housing costs, wealth, debt, and people without jobs are outside this wage table, so it is too narrow to settle the broader shape of the recovery.

The next table has to be read across the 25th percentile, the real median, and part-time pay to show whether one quarter became a trend.

BLS earnings release schedule

Three lines matter on October 21: whether the 25th percentile again outgrows the 75th, whether the real median rises above $374, and whether part-time pay rebounds. If all three move together, the policy-success case becomes stronger. If only the first survives, the post will have moved from accurate arithmetic to a conclusion larger than the data.

In three lines
  • BLS data show lower-paid workers had the faster wage growth.
  • Median pay also rose faster than prices.
  • The data do not prove that policy caused the gain.
