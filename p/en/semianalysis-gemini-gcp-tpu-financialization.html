Why Did Google Split DeepMind's Long-Term Research from Gemini Delivery?

Open in the Stacks app → Read the original ↗

Sundar Pichai announced a new operating structure for DeepMind: Demis Hassabis will focus on long-term frontier AI and scientific research, while Koray Kavukcuoglu will lead Gemini engineering, the app, and developer services.

Hassabis becomes Chair of DeepMind and Chief Scientist of Alphabet, while Kavukcuoglu takes day-to-day leadership and Gemini product delivery
출처: blog.google

To understand why that matters, it helps to start with how Google ended up with its current AI organization.

Two research teams inside Google

DeepMind was founded in Britain in 2010. Google acquired it in 2014, but the lab continued to operate from London with its own research culture. Google also had a separate internal AI research team. DeepMind began as an outside company that Google bought; the other research team was built inside Google. Different origins and leaders kept the two groups separate even after they belonged to the same company. The arrival of ChatGPT in late 2022 changed the pressure on Google. Publishing research was no longer enough; new AI had to become a product and reach users quickly. Google combined the teams into Google DeepMind in 2023. The goal was to put researchers and computing resources in one organization so decisions and product work could move faster.

Research and products under one roof

After the merger, DeepMind's job became much larger. It had to research new AI, build Gemini, and help put the system into Search, mobile apps, and business products. Gemini is the AI program that understands a request and produces an answer. Turning it into a service also requires apps, bug fixes, and enough servers to respond quickly when many people use it. Coordination with Google Cloud became central. Google Cloud is the business where companies pay to use Google's servers and AI, so one leader was overseeing both long-term research and products that had to ship now.

Why Google split the roles

Google did not disclose a specific trigger for the change. At the time, however, a major new Gemini model was running behind its planned release and several people who had led Gemini development were leaving.

Reuters reported that the delayed model launch and senior departures formed the backdrop to Hassabis taking long-term research while Kavukcuoglu assumed daily operations and commercialization ↗

Hassabis will serve as Chair of DeepMind and Chief Scientist of Alphabet. He will concentrate on broad, human-level AI and scientific work while continuing to run the AI drug-discovery company he leads. Kavukcuoglu will run DeepMind day to day. Model engineering, the Gemini app, and developer products will sit under his operational leadership. Hassabis is not leaving DeepMind. He will set the long-range research direction, while Kavukcuoglu is responsible for turning research into Gemini and Google Cloud products.

More than a people reshuffle

AI development needs researchers, specialized chips, servers, and data centers. The industry calls the processing capacity supplied by that equipment compute. That capacity is limited. Servers assigned to long-term experiments cannot simultaneously serve a Gemini launch or an outside customer. SemiAnalysis turns this constraint into an investment question. Selling Google's own TPU AI chips to outside labs such as Anthropic can increase Google Cloud revenue, but it can also reduce the spare capacity available to Gemini. SemiAnalysis estimates that more than 20% of TPU shipments from the third quarter of 2026 through the fourth quarter of 2027 will be sold directly to Anthropic. It also models more than $73 billion of annualized third-party AI infrastructure revenue in 2027, but that is an analyst estimate, not Alphabet guidance.

Is the spending producing results?

Alphabet spent $44.9 billion on servers, data centers, and other capital projects in the second quarter of 2026. Cash left after operating the business and making those investments was negative $5.9 billion. In the same quarter, Google Cloud revenue reached $24.8 billion, up 82% from a year earlier, and operating income reached $8.8 billion. The investment bill and the Google Cloud payoff are rising together.

The reorganization will succeed if Kavukcuoglu can speed up Gemini releases and turn the larger server bill into Google Cloud revenue and profit. If Gemini ships on schedule while Google Cloud revenue and profit continue to grow, the split will look productive. If investment keeps rising while launches keep slipping, changing the reporting lines will not have solved the problem. The central issue is not the DeepMind organization chart itself. It is how Google divides people, budgets, and AI servers between long-term research and products that must earn money now. The effects should appear over time in Gemini release speed, Google Cloud revenue and profit, and the cash left after building more servers and data centers.

In three lines
  • Google has put long-horizon DeepMind research and the delivery of Gemini products under different leaders.
  • The change can affect not only people, but also how limited AI servers and budgets are divided between research and customer-facing cloud services.
  • Gemini release speed, Google Cloud revenue and profit, and cash left after investment will show the result over time.
