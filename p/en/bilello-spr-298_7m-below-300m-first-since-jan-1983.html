US gasoline is back above $4, and the emergency oil reserve is back at 1983 levels

The US Strategic Petroleum Reserve is now at its lowest level since January 1983. Over the past 5 years we've seen a drawdown of 322 million barrels, a 52% decline.

August 14, 2026

The oil the United States keeps buried for emergencies is down to 298.7 million barrels. It has not been below 300 million since January 1983.

Weekly Petroleum Status Report (US Energy Information Administration, published 2026-08-12 for the week ending August 7) ↗
SPR, week ending August 7298.7 million barrels
Same week a year earlier403.2 million barrels
Designed storage capacity714 million barrels

More than a hundred million barrels left in a single year, and the caverns are less than half full. The maximum drawdown rate is 4.4 million barrels a day, so what remains is roughly two months of it.

EIA Weekly Petroleum Status Report for the week ending 2026-08-07 and DOE SPR Quick Facts as of 2026-08-05. Retrieved 2026-08-16.

The Strategic Petroleum Reserve is crude stored in 61 salt caverns at four sites on the Gulf Coast. When war or a supply break leaves the market short, the president opens it and lets barrels out.

The reserve has been the price tool

Oil has been climbing since the Strait of Hormuz effectively closed in late February, and this store of crude is what Washington reached for.

US strategic oil stockpile nears four-decade low as Iran war drags on (Al-Monitor, 2026-06-11)
출처: al-monitor.com

That report noted the reserve draining steadily as the war stretched on. In the two months since, it has fallen further and set a fresh four-decade low. On August 14 West Texas Intermediate settled at $82.40 a barrel and Brent at $88.52. Gasoline averaged $4.07 a gallon nationally on August 15. Barrels went into the market from the reserve, and prices are still here.

Lent out rather than bought back

Here is the piece the original post leaves out. There are two ways for the government to put crude back in the caverns. One is a purchase, paid for out of budget. The other is an exchange, where refiners take barrels now and return barrels later.

  • January 7, 1983

    294.8 million barrels, back when the reserve was still being built up

  • January 2010

    726.6 million barrels, the all-time peak

  • November 12, 2025

    The Energy Department awards contracts to buy back 1 million barrels

  • June 10, 2026

    Fifth solicitation under the 172-million-barrel release, repaid in barrels rather than cash

It took 27 years to fill. Emptying it did not take nearly as long.

EIA weekly stocks series and US Department of Energy press releases dated 2025-11-12 and 2026-06-10. Retrieved 2026-08-16.

The last confirmed cash purchase was in November 2025, for 1 million barrels. Set against the capacity of the caverns, that is a corner of one room. An exchange is a different animal. The solicitation posted on June 10 covered 40 million barrels, with refiners taking crude now and returning crude later. In earlier exchanges, borrowers repaid roughly 26% more than they took. From the lender's side, it is a way to refill without spending a dollar of budget. From the borrower's side, it is a bet that expensive oil today will be cheaper oil on the day the barrels have to go back. Which means refilling the reserve now rides on the future oil price rather than on an appropriation.

The 125 days the Energy Department cited

The mechanical numbers are simple. Even after a presidential order it takes 13 days for the crude to reach the market, and no more than 4.4 million barrels a day can be pulled out however urgent it gets. Members of the International Energy Agency are required to hold reserves equal to 90 days of net imports. The Energy Department said that at 411 million barrels, where the reserve stood at the end of 2025, the SPR alone covered 125 days. How many days it covers at today's level is not something published data shows. The split is whether the barrels that went out on exchange come back as barrels. If they do, the United States refills the caverns without spending tax money and gets its instrument back before the next supply shock. If they do not, or if oil spikes again first, the country meets that moment with nothing left to sell into the market. The question then is not what the price is. It is whether the government still has anything to put against it.

In three lines
  • The US Strategic Petroleum Reserve is down to 298.7 million barrels, its first reading below 300 million since January 1983.
  • On the surface that is the cost of using the reserve to hold prices down, and gasoline is still above $4 a gallon.
  • But the way the government is refilling it now is not a budgeted purchase. It is an exchange, lending barrels out and taking barrels back.
