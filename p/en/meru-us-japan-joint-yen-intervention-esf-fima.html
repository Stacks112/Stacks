On July 31 the New York Fed sold euros to buy yen

Open in the Stacks app → Read the original ↗

On July 31 the Federal Reserve Bank of New York sold euros and bought yen on behalf of the US Treasury. It is the first time since 2011, fifteen years, that the United States has entered the currency market on the buying side of the yen. Bloomberg reported that Japan alone spent 8.45 trillion yen, about $52.8 billion, on July 30, the day before. Observers put that at the largest single day Tokyo has ever spent. The yen closed Friday at 157.40 to the dollar, its strongest since early May. What the United States added is less than a fifth of that. What will make this intervention worth remembering is not the amount but the way the dollars were made.

A single memo caught in a Reuters photo

It started with one piece of paper. On July 31 a Reuters photograph from a cabinet meeting caught Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent's memo in full. Under the words 'to do' was the line 'buy $5-10bn of yen'. Reuters reported that the Treasury had warned banks that morning that an intervention was possible. The Treasury did not respond on the contents of the memo. That there was neither a denial nor a confirmation read, in the market, as confirmation.

To buy yen you first need dollars

Making the yen stronger means selling dollars and buying yen. That requires having the dollars first, and where those dollars come from is the heart of this episode. The money Japan uses on the exchange rate comes from the Foreign Exchange Fund Special Account run by the Ministry of Finance. It holds about $1.3747 trillion, and $1.0087 trillion of that, most of what can be turned into cash immediately, is US Treasuries. Japan's dollar wallet is filled with Treasuries. Opening the wallet means selling them, and the moment they are sold the market has more of them, prices fall and yields rise. And the indicator Bessent has repeatedly said he watches most closely is the 10-year Treasury yield. Selling Treasuries to hold up the yen pushes US yields up. That is the problem this intervention had to solve first.

The ESF and FIMA, two wallets that leave Treasuries alone

What the United States sold this time was not dollars but euros. The Treasury keeps a separate pocket called the Exchange Stabilization Fund, and it holds euros and yen. Selling euros to buy yen touches not a single Treasury. What Japan will use is not yet confirmed. The original post expects Japan to use the FIMA repo facility. Created in March 2020, it lets a central bank pledge the Treasuries it already keeps at the New York Fed as collateral, borrow dollars for a while and buy them back later.

Federal Reserve announces the establishment of a standing FIMA repo facility (Federal Reserve, 2021-07-28)
출처: federalreserve.gov

The Fed's announcement pins the collateral to Treasuries held in custody at the New York Fed and the per-country limit to $60 billion. Selling a Treasury and pledging one are entirely different things in the market. Selling puts the paper on the market; pledging does not.

$52.8bn in a day against a $60bn window

Japan spent $52.8 billion in a single day on July 30. The per-country limit on FIMA repo is $60 billion. Drawing the window once, all the way, barely covers one of those days. For this facility to be a way of holding on without selling Treasuries, it has to be borrowed and repaid over and over, and the dollars for repayment have to come from somewhere in the end. Pledging as collateral may not remove Treasury selling so much as push it back. If the yen turns on its own inside the time bought, it ends as time bought. If it does not, it arrives all at once, in proportion to the delay.

The single line that prints on Thursday, August 6

The Fed publishes its balance sheet every Thursday. One line on it records the dollars foreign monetary authorities have borrowed against Treasuries. That line reads zero right now. If a number other than zero prints on August 6, Japan found its dollars without selling Treasuries. If it stays at zero while the yen slips again, Japan went back to the old method or stopped intervening. Where the yen goes from 157 will be in the news all week. Nobody will write up that one line on Thursday. It is the second of the two that will still be there in a few years.

In three lines
  • What this intervention is worth checking for is not how far the yen went, but the way the United States produced dollars without selling Treasuries.
  • The United States sold euros held in the ESF. If Japan uses FIMA repo, no Treasuries reach the market.
  • But FIMA's $60 billion per-country limit barely clears the $52.8 billion Japan spent in one day. It can be a way of delaying, not a way of removing.
