The inside story of how Samsung is lifting DRAM output 15% at Hwaseong without a new fab

Open in the Stacks app → Read the original ↗

Samsung Electronics is consolidating its DRAM production process at its Hwaseong campus, aiming to raise commodity DRAM capacity by about 15% from the start of the year by year-end. Chip analyst Jukan relayed the plan on July 27, citing Korean industry sources.

What exactly is an end-fab

Chipmaking splits into two broad stages. The main fab etches transistors and circuits onto a wafer, and the end-fab connects those components into a finished chip. The end-fab is the last step of front-end processing, where the memory becomes something that can actually be used. Samsung's Memory Manufacturing Technology Center formed a task force this month to build a new commodity DRAM end-fab at H1, the first complex of its Hwaseong campus. End-fab equipment scattered across the second complex (H2) and the Cheonan campus is being relocated to H1, and new production lines are going into the space that frees up.

Why the rush now

Samsung had historically expanded its main fabs at Hwaseong while leaving some end-fab work behind in Cheonan. The physical distance between the two stages stretched out how long it took to get finished DRAM out the door, which became a drag on ramping up commodity supply. This reshuffle reuses idle cleanroom space at H1 that opened up after older memory lines were dismantled. Repurposing an existing cleanroom instead of building a new one cuts both the capital spend and the construction timeline. Jun Young-hyun, who heads Samsung's Device Solutions division, reviewed how to use that space and settled on making it the new end-fab hub, essentially importing the model Samsung already runs at Pyeongtaek, where main fabs and end-fabs have been colocated since the campus was built.

What this means for Samsung

An industry official said Samsung's commodity DRAM capacity should be about 15% higher by year-end than it was in January, with the consolidated layout also improving logistics since wafers can move to downstream lines in bulk. The move lands as DDR4 prices have jumped more than 80% in the first half of the year. With the memory shortage severe enough that major customers like Apple are weighing Chinese DRAM suppliers, Samsung's own capacity expansion reads as an attempt to meet that demand directly rather than cede it to cheaper outside supply.

What settles it

The question comes down to one thing. Does 15% more supply ease the DRAM squeeze, or does demand stay ahead? Choosing in-house expansion over cheap external sourcing is itself a sign Samsung does not expect this squeeze to be short. Year-end commodity DRAM prices are the tell. If prices hold even after the new supply lands, demand runs that deep. If they roll over, the peak of the squeeze has passed.

In three lines
  • Samsung is consolidating DRAM production at Hwaseong to lift capacity about 15% by year-end versus January.
  • Instead of new fabs it is refitting cleanrooms freed by retiring old lines, saving both capex and construction time.
  • With DDR4 up more than 80% in the first half alone, how much this extra supply eases the squeeze is the thing to watch.
