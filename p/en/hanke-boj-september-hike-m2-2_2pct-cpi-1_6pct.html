Why would the Bank of Japan hike again with inflation under target for a fifth month?

Steve Hanke@steve_hanke

Markets are pricing in an 80% chance that the Bank of Japan will boost interest rates in September.

Meanwhile, the money supply (M2) is growing at an anemic 2.2%/yr. More monetary tightening is just what Japan does not need.

BOJ = DIGGING ITSELF A DEEPER HOLE.

August 17, 2026

Markets expect the Bank of Japan to raise rates once more in September. Reuters reported on August 14 that the bank is weighing a September hike and a faster pace of tightening, and that markets were pricing in nearly an 80% chance of a move that month.

BOJ eyeing September rate hike, faster pace of tightening, sources say (Reuters, 2026-08-14)
출처: finance.yahoo.com

The two numbers usually cited as grounds for a hike are both sitting under target.

Money is growing slowly and they are tightening anyway

Policy rate1.00%
Money stock (M2), year on year2.2%
Core CPI, year on year1.6%

Both numbers a hiker would normally lean on point the wrong way. Money is growing slower than the 2.6% average for 2000 to 2020 that Hanke cited in his July column, and core inflation has been under the 2% target for five straight months. His premise, that tightening is not what Japan needs right now, checks out in the data.

Japan June core inflation accelerates, stays below BOJ target (Reuters, 2026-07-23)
출처: finance.yahoo.com

Reuters reported that core inflation rose to 1.6% in June from 1.4% in May while still coming in under the 2% target for a fifth month. The national figure for July has not been published yet. The money stock is simply the total amount of money circulating in an economy. M2 counts the part of it people can spend right away, cash and deposits, and when that number grows slowly it means there is less force available to push prices up. Raise rates from here and money that was already growing slowly grows slower still. That is why Hanke wrote that more tightening is exactly what Japan does not need.

Eighteen months took the rate from 0.25% to 1.00%. A fourth hike is hanging on September.

Retrieved 2026-08-17 · Bank of Japan policy statements, English releases

What has moved is not the price data. It is the yen. Japan intervened alongside the United States last month, and the yen was still at 159.1 to the dollar on August 11. A cheaper yen makes imported goods dearer, and that cost reaches consumer prices with a lag. At his late-July press conference Governor Ueda said the effect of currency swings on inflation may be larger than it used to be. In the same appearance he said underlying inflation is nearing 2% and that the bank must watch upside risks in a way it did not before.

Wages cleared 5% for a third year

The other input is pay. Rengo, Japan's trade union confederation, finalized this year's spring wage round on July 3 at an average increase of 5.01%, the third consecutive year above 5%.

Japan Unions Clinch 5.01 Pct Wage Hikes in 2026 Shunto (Jiji Press via nippon.com, 2026-07-03) ↗

Rising pay gives households more to spend, and that spending lifts prices. This is the picture the bank has waited years to see. It has not shown up in the price data yet.

Hanke's reading

Money is already growing at just 2.2% a year. Tighten from here and you shrink the force that could lift prices at all.

VS
The bank's reading

Core inflation is still low, but a weak yen and a third year of 5% pay rises will push prices up from here.

Two readings of one meeting. One is looking at money that is already circulating slowly, the other at inflation that has not arrived.

September 18 answers it

If the meeting on September 17 and 18 takes the rate to 1.25%, the bank will have moved on the currency and on wages rather than on realized inflation. The next question then shifts to the money stock: whether money growing at around 2% a year can keep supporting a third straight year of 5% pay increases. If the bank holds instead, five months of below-target inflation will have beaten the 80% the market priced in. Attention then moves to the July national CPI due on August 21. Raising rates in a country where money circulates slowly looks less like an act of price control and more like an act of currency defense.

In three lines
  • Markets put the odds near 80% that the Bank of Japan lifts its 1.00% policy rate again at the September 17-18 meeting.
  • On the surface that reads as a hike aimed at inflation. Yet core CPI has been under the 2% target for five months and money is growing at 2.2% a year, slower than the pre-pandemic average.
  • What is left as grounds is not the price data. It is a yen near 159 to the dollar and a third straight year of pay rises above 5%.
